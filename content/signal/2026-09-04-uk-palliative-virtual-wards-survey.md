A cross-sectional online survey using a multilevel, snowballing methodology gathered 86 responses covering 22 palliative virtual wards across the UK — the first aggregated national data on this model [Audit/Data] (May-Miller & Swann, DOI: 10.1136/spcare-2025-005482). All responding services supported patients with unstable palliative symptoms who would otherwise need hospital or hospice admission. Telephone contact (100%) and face-to-face contact (93%) were near-universal; video consultation (69%) and remote-monitoring technology (15%) were used by a minority. Three-quarters of services depend on charitable funding, and two virtual wards have already closed for lack of funds.

The survey reports that professionals perceive these services as reducing hospital admissions and supporting patient choice, but the authors themselves note this is a self-reported perception from providers, not a comparative outcomes study against standard care, and call for further research into comparative benefit.

For any service planning or funding a palliative virtual ward, this is the first national comparator for what current UK practice actually looks like: predominantly low-technology, staff-contact-intensive, and financially precarious rather than technology-substituted care.
