A scoping review with narrative synthesis, covering 28 peer-reviewed records and 7 official sources searched to 21 May 2026, examined rural telehealth and virtual care for rural, remote, frontier and Indigenous communities in Western Canada and the Western United States. It found moderate confidence that telehealth reduces travel and enables specialist econsultation or telementoring; low-to-moderate confidence for hybrid primary care and telemental health; and only low confidence for durable reductions in emergency department use.

[Synthesis] As a scoping review with narrative synthesis rather than a meta-analysis, this study grades the strength of the underlying evidence rather than pooling a single effect size, and its confidence gradings vary considerably by use case. Its explicit conclusion is that telehealth is most appropriately implemented as a hybrid, locally anchored access model, not as a stand-alone substitute for in-person care — a second, independent source now reporting low confidence specifically for durable reductions in emergency department use from remote and telehealth models.

For services planning remote-monitoring or telehealth expansion into rural or dispersed populations, this is a counterweight to reading rising remote-technology uptake alone as proof that technology closes the distance gap: the evidence base to date supports telehealth as a hybrid complement to local, in-person care rather than a replacement for it.
