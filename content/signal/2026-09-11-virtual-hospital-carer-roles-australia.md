According to PubMed, a multi-method triangulation study of an Australian acute respiratory virtual hospital model analysed 3,282 patient-reported experience measure surveys alongside 52 semi-structured interviews with patients, carers and healthcare workers, mapping their accounts across admission, treatment and discharge ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1111/hex.70654); Ward et al.). Virtual hospital care required patients and carers to take on new technical and digital support roles — onboarding, equipment use, troubleshooting — while their existing logistical, household and clinical support roles expanded in scope [Primary Study].

The distributional finding is the one that matters. Carers acted as extensions or substitutes for patient responsibilities specifically where patients had low digital literacy, language barriers or reduced mobility. Participants valued the convenience, safety and responsiveness of virtual care; inconsistent communication, technology usability problems and insufficient support for carers were what produced poor experiences.

This is a qualitative and survey-based study of one named service, so it cannot quantify how much unpaid work transfers, or establish that the same distribution holds in another system. What it does establish, in a service already operating at scale rather than a pilot, is the direction of that transfer: the work of monitoring and self-management does not disappear when the bed does, and it lands hardest on exactly the patients least equipped to carry it — and therefore on their carers.

Read against the use-value standard, that is a cost the model's own evaluation boundary does not capture. A virtual ward business case measures the bed released. It does not measure the carer hours that replace it, and this study is direct evidence from inside a functioning service that those hours are real, unevenly distributed, and concentrated among the digitally excluded. Any service extending a home-based model toward a dispersed or deprived population should treat carer capacity and digital access as first-order design requirements with named support attached, not as acceptability questions to be surveyed after deployment.
