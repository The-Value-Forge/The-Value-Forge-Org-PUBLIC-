The National Audit Office's report on hospital patient flow, published 9 September 2026, concludes that flow through hospitals in England "is not working properly" [Audit/Data]. On an average 24-hour snapshot in May 2026, 2,241 patients were waiting in A&E corridors and 669 in ward corridors across England. Half a million people waited more than 24 hours in A&E over the past year. Delayed discharge was costed at an estimated £2bn between August 2025 and April 2026.

This is a national audit of system performance rather than a clinical study, and it carries the strengths and limits of that design: the figures are national in scope and drawn from operational reporting rather than a research cohort, so they describe the scale of the problem precisely without testing any intervention against a comparator.

What makes the report consequential is what it locates as the constraint. The British Geriatrics Society's response, published the same day, calls for a systems approach — stronger community services to reduce emergency attendance, front-door frailty diversion to community care or same-day discharge, and discharge planning that begins at admission rather than at the point of medical fitness. Neither document identifies a gap in the underlying clinical evidence for those interventions. Both locate the gap in whether they are consistently delivered.

That is the same mechanism the evidence base has been describing qualitatively for weeks, now carrying national quantification: outcomes track the consistency of a model's implementation and coordination, not the strength of the evidence beneath it. A report of this kind cannot establish that community capacity would absorb the demand it describes — it did not test that — and any service reading it should treat the systems prescription as the direction the audit points rather than as a costed, evidenced plan. The value of the numbers is that they establish the size of the prize, and they establish it at national scale.
