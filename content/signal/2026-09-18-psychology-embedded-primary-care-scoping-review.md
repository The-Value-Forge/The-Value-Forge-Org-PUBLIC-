A UK scoping review of 72 studies, searched from 2010, examined the views and experiences of patients and professionals where psychology provision is co-located or integrated within primary care. Patients and staff consistently described such arrangements positively: improved access to psychological support, greater patient knowledge and skills, and reduced workload and stress for primary care staff. Psychology professionals working in these embedded roles, however, reported role-demand pressures, organisational challenges and unmet training needs of their own.

[Synthesis] The review's most consequential finding is not the benefit itself but its absence at national scale: a standing recommendation from the British Psychological Society for routine embedding of psychology in primary care remains unimplemented. As a scoping review of qualitative and mixed experience, it maps what is described rather than measuring a clinical-outcome effect size, so the benefits recorded here are about access, knowledge and staff experience rather than symptom or diagnostic outcomes.

For primary care networks serving high-deprivation, high-demand populations, this is evidence that embedding psychology capacity directly — rather than relying solely on referral pathways into secondary mental health services — is associated with better-described patient and staff experience, provided the workforce and training pressures on embedded psychology professionals themselves are also planned for.
